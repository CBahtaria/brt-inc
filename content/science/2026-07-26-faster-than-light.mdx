No physical object, message, or information can travel faster than the speed of light in a vacuum — **299,792,458 metres per second**. This is a structural fact about spacetime, not an engineering limitation. At *c*, time stops for the traveller. Beyond *c*, causality breaks: effects would precede their causes, and you could in principle receive a message before it was sent.

Einstein established this in 1905. Every precision measurement since has confirmed it.

But four phenomena create a convincing illusion of faster-than-light behaviour. Understanding each one precisely is more interesting than wishful thinking about warp drives.

---

## 1. Cherenkov Radiation — the optical sonic boom

Light slows down inside materials. In water it travels at about 75% of *c*; in dense glass, closer to 66%. Charged particles (electrons, protons) moving through these media can exceed the *local* speed of light without violating Special Relativity, because they remain below *c* in vacuum.

The result is a electromagnetic shockwave — the optical equivalent of a sonic boom. This is **Cherenkov radiation**: the eerie blue glow you see in nuclear reactor cooling pools. It is also the operating principle behind Cherenkov detectors used in particle physics.

No physics is broken. The particles never exceed *c* in vacuum.

---

## 2. The Expanding Universe — space moves, objects don't

Galaxies beyond roughly 14 billion light-years are receding from us faster than *c* as measured by cosmological redshift. This appears to violate the speed limit until you understand what is actually moving.

The galaxies themselves are not speeding through space. The metric of space — the fabric of spacetime — is expanding between us and them. Special Relativity's limit governs motion *through* space, not the expansion of space itself.

These galaxies sit beyond our cosmological event horizon: light they emit today will never reach us, stretched to infinite redshift by the expansion. They are within our observable universe but causally disconnected from our future.

---

## 3. Quantum Entanglement — instantaneous but informationless

Two entangled particles share a quantum state. Measuring one collapses the state of the other — instantaneously, at any distance. Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance" and used it as an argument against quantum mechanics being a complete theory. Bell's theorem (1964) and subsequent experiments (Aspect, 1982; Zeilinger, 2022 Nobel Prize) confirmed the entanglement is real.

The key: the collapse is **random**. You cannot choose what result you get, so you cannot encode a message in it. To verify the correlation, you must classically compare notes with the other party — a communication channel limited to ≤*c*.

The no-communication theorem makes this rigorous. Entanglement cannot transmit information faster than light.

---

## 4. Superluminal Shadows and Laser Spots

Sweep a laser pointer across the face of the Moon. The illuminated spot moves at roughly 50,000 km/s — far above *c*. Nothing is broken.

No photon travels that path. The spot is a geometric intersection: successive photons hit successive points on the Moon. It is precisely analogous to a shadow cast on a distant screen by a rapidly rotating object. The *pattern* moves faster than light; no matter, energy, or information does.

---

## Why These Loopholes Don't Help

All four phenomena share a property: **no information moves faster than light through them**.

This is the deeper principle. FTL communication would violate causality — in some reference frames, the signal would arrive before it was sent. That leads to logical contradictions (you could signal your own past to prevent yourself from sending the signal). Causality violations are not tolerated by any consistent physical theory.

Theoretical constructs like the Alcubierre warp drive avoid this by warping space rather than moving through it at FTL velocity, but they require exotic negative-energy matter with no known physical realisation.
