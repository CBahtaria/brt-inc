The systems deciding who gets hired, who gets bail, and who gets credit are increasingly algorithmic — and increasingly opaque. Across the United States and Europe, the accountability failures of these systems are at least documented. In SADC, the same tools are being procured without the institutional infrastructure needed to contest their outputs.

## COMPAS and the Recidivism Scoring Precedent

COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions) assigns defendants a recidivism risk score used by judges during sentencing and bail hearings. A 2016 ProPublica investigation established that the system's error rates differed significantly by race: Black defendants were approximately twice as likely to be incorrectly flagged as high risk compared to white defendants who reoffended at similar rates. Northpointe (now Equivant) disputed the methodology; the academic debate is ongoing. What is not in dispute is that COMPAS produces a score, a defendant cannot inspect the algorithm that generated it, and judges in multiple jurisdictions cited those scores without knowing what they were weighting.

The due process gap is structural. A defendant facing a high-risk score cannot call the model's training data as evidence. There is no right to algorithmic explanation in the jurisdictions where COMPAS operates. The score enters the courtroom as a fact and leaves as a sentencing input.

## Hiring Algorithms and the Amazon Precedent

In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon had developed and then scrapped a machine learning recruiting tool after discovering it was systematically downgrading CVs containing the word "women's" — as in "women's chess club" — and penalising graduates of all-women's colleges. The model had been trained on a decade of historical hiring decisions. Those decisions reflected the male-dominant composition of Amazon's technical workforce. The model learned to replicate the bias embedded in its training signal.

Amazon's engineering team caught the problem and shelved the system before deployment at scale. Most organisations procuring similar tools do not have comparable internal review capacity. HireVue, a company offering AI-driven video interview analysis, deploys facial expression analysis, vocal prosody scoring, and word-choice assessment as inputs into candidate ranking. These systems are in active deployment. Their training data provenance and error-rate distributions by demographic group are not published.

## SADC Procurement Without Explainability Mandates

SADC member states are beginning to procure risk-scoring and decision-support tools for justice, social services, and public-sector hiring. Unlike the European Union's AI Act — which classifies high-risk AI applications (including those affecting employment and justice) as requiring conformity assessments, transparency documentation, and human-review mechanisms — there is no equivalent mandate in force across the region.

An institution in Eswatini, Zambia, or Tanzania that procures a scoring tool from an international vendor is under no legal obligation to receive, publish, or maintain algorithmic documentation. Applicants and defendants subject to those scores have no statutory right to explanation. The appeals surface does not exist because the obligation to create it was never imposed.

Eswatini's institutional scale makes this asymmetry acute. The kingdom's population is approximately 1.2 million. A single biased algorithm applied to civil service recruitment or social grant eligibility affects a proportionally large share of the affected population in absolute terms — and the pool of individuals with the technical capacity to identify or challenge the bias is smaller than in larger economies. The concentration of institutional harm from a single miscalibrated system is higher, not lower.

## What a Defensible Deployment Looks Like

Explainability requirements belong in procurement contracts, not afterthoughts. Any scoring system whose output influences a decision affecting individual rights — employment, credit, legal status — should require a vendor-provided explanation capability: specifically, which input features drove a given score, and what the system's false-positive and false-negative rates are by demographic group.

Human-review mechanisms are not optional for high-stakes decisions. Automated scoring can inform a decision; it should not constitute one without a human authority capable of being held accountable for the outcome.

Audit trails must be maintained for the life of the affected decision. If an AI system contributed to a hiring or sentencing decision, that contribution must be logged in a way that is retrievable during any subsequent review or challenge.

The institutional capacity to enforce these standards exists in SADC — the SADC Secretariat, national human rights commissions, and ministries of justice have mandates that extend to procedural fairness. The gap is in technical awareness and procurement standard-setting. Closing it requires specificity about what algorithmic accountability means in practice, not just a commitment to "responsible AI" as a phrase.
