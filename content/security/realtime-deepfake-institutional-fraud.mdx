The deepfake threat has passed the latency threshold that matters. Voice synthesis pipelines can produce output in under 200 milliseconds — below the conversational pause threshold where a speaker would notice something is wrong. Face-swap models running on consumer-grade GPU hardware produce convincing output at 30 frames per second, sufficient for live video calls. The capability to conduct real-time, live impersonation of a known individual now exists outside controlled laboratory conditions and inside accessible tooling.

## The Latency Problem: Why 200ms Is the Threshold

Human conversational timing research establishes that response latencies under approximately 250ms are perceived as natural conversation. Above that threshold, subjects begin to perceive the interaction as oddly paced. A voice synthesis system with 200ms end-to-end latency — from audio input to synthesised audio output — can conduct a live phone or video call that feels temporally natural to the target.

Modern text-to-speech and voice conversion architectures (real-time extensions of models in the VITS, XTTS, and related families) have reached this threshold with fine-tuned models. The input requirement is modest: approximately 3–10 minutes of clean target audio is sufficient for a voice conversion model to reproduce the prosodic characteristics, vocal texture, and speaking rate of a specific individual. That amount of audio is available for virtually any senior official who has participated in a recorded public meeting, broadcast interview, or conference presentation.

## The Hong Kong Fraud Case and the State of Play

In early 2024, a finance worker at a multinational firm in Hong Kong was defrauded of approximately $25 million USD after being directed to make transfers by individuals impersonating company executives in a video conference call. The deepfake models used were of sufficient quality that the worker, who was initially suspicious of an earlier phishing email, became convinced of the legitimacy of the call after seeing and hearing the familiar faces and voices of colleagues.

This case is significant not because of its financial scale but because of its operational characteristics: it used live video, not pre-recorded content; it involved multiple deepfake personas simultaneously in a group call; and it required no access to the company's internal systems — the attack surface was entirely the human participant's trust in audiovisual authenticity.

The detection false-negative rate of current commercial deepfake detectors against state-of-the-art generative models is materially non-zero. Detector performance degrades under compression (video conferencing codecs introduce compression artefacts that mask generative artefacts) and under adversarial prompting (model outputs can be post-processed to defeat specific detector signatures). Detection is a viable layer but not a sufficient one.

## Court Evidence and the Fabrication Risk

The evidentiary implications extend beyond fraud. Video and audio evidence introduced in legal proceedings is subject to authentication standards that vary significantly across SADC jurisdictions. In many courts, the authentication standard for a recording is met by a witness who attests that the recording accurately represents what was said — a standard created in an era when such attestation was reliable because the cost and capability required to fabricate a convincing recording at scale was prohibitive.

That cost and capability assumption is no longer valid. A recording of a defendant confessing, an official making a corrupt offer, or a sensitive briefing being disclosed — all of these are now within the fabrication capability of a moderately resourced adversary. The evidentiary standard that treats video and audio as inherently more reliable than testimonial evidence was not designed for this environment.

SADC governments that are parties to criminal proceedings involving digital evidence need authentication infrastructure: cryptographic chain-of-custody for recordings from the moment of capture, metadata integrity verification, and forensic analysis capacity that can evaluate deepfake probability at a level appropriate for evidentiary challenge.

## SADC Government Video-Call Authentication Gap

Video conferencing is now standard for inter-agency briefings, cabinet-level communications, and diplomatic interactions across SADC. The authentication mechanisms in use — login credentials to a conferencing platform — authenticate the account, not the person. An adversary with access to an official's account credentials, or who has established a parallel call that the target has been redirected to, can conduct a deepfake impersonation without defeating any authentication gate.

The mitigation architecture has three components. Out-of-band session verification for high-stakes calls: a second-channel confirmation (encrypted message to a pre-registered device, or a verbal challenge response using a pre-shared phrase) before the substantive call begins. This is operationally feasible and requires no specialised technology. Institutional policy limiting transfer authorisations, disclosure approvals, and binding commitments made solely via video call, without independent written confirmation through a separately authenticated channel. Deepfake detection tooling as a detection layer on call recording infrastructure, with alerts for calls exhibiting detection signals above a threshold — not as a hard gate, but as a flag for human review.

The threat is not speculative. The tooling is available. The institutional preparation required to resist it is modest — the primary barrier is awareness that the authentication surface that institutions currently rely on is insufficient for the capability level now in the hands of motivated adversaries.
